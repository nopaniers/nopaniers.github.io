Recently I read [this
article](https://www.elysian.press/p/employee-ownership) which
controversially suggests that "Every company should be owned by its
employees". It's an interesting article which presents Employee Stock
Ownership Plans (ESOPs) as an alternative to prohibitative tax
schemes, and as a method for ensuring that people working for a
company, fully benefit from the work that they're doing. 

It is ironic to see what is basically a Marxist idea, that the means
of production should be owned by the workers, springing up in an
staunchly pro-capitalist setting, being described as "It's capitalism
at its best" and shared on venture capital sites. Fortunately, apart
from identifying a common problem the solution is very much open maket
and almost the opposite of rejection of private ownership and state
control promoted by communism.

I like the idea put forward. I do think that we should make it the
norm that companies surrender part of the ownership of that company to
the workers who create their wealth, and I do not think this benefit
should be restricted to the upper management.

[Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41065227) had
several comments, but the leading one was this:

> The reason not every company does it to all its employees is
> probably that for those employees, it wouldn't affect incentives
> much and it would make payment subject to the vagaries of the stock
> market. Your barista at Starbucks is not going to increase the stock
> price no matter how well he fills your order; at the same time,
> maybe he wants to know how much he takes home every day.

> If the story is "it should be the law that every company offers
> stock options", then that would be a dumb law for the reasons above.

The commenter's argument, if I can steelman it, is that employees won't be
motivated by being given stock options, because nothing they can
individually do will affect the share price. So offering employee
stock options does nothing to give employees an incentive to work
harder, so why do it?

The article gave examples of the opposite happening (ie. employees
actually being motivated by being granted ownership). It's not hard to
imagine that workers are not solely driven by profit, but by having
influence in the company they work for, or working for a company that
strives to provide better worker conditions rather than just
profit. For example, it describes the way in which employees having
ownership can automatically leads to a better company culture and more
respect shown to employees:

> When the C-Suite goes into work every day, they see the
> receptionist, the person on the factory floor, the guy who's
> building the building or digging the ditches, and they know that
> those are the shareholders they are responsible for. They take that
> seriously.

Personally, thinking as a worker, I believe would make a significant
difference. If I had part ownership in the company that I'm working
for, that would come with a dignity which has nothing whatever to do
with the share price. One of the problems, in the modern world, is how
disconnected we are with the results of our labour. This problem
infamously drove the
[unabomber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Kaczynski) utterly mad,
and is a very common one to hear about in the nine-to-five
grind. Granting partial ownership is one obvious way to repair at
least some of that, and to reconnect us with the point of getting out
of bed in the morning.

However the commenter is technically right in this respect: Starbucks
is a huge company, so, a barista's behaviour isn't behaviour isn't
going to shift the stock price. Conversely, Starbucks is a huge
company, so granting part ownership to an individual barista isn't
going to shift the share price - and so providing this benefit
wouldn't have an adverse effect on others.

An individual doesn't affect the stock price much, but as a group,
baristas absolutely affect the stock price and the perception of the
company. Baristas are key to Starbucks success. Like all companies,
Starbucks relies on the hard work of these employees. If tomorrow,
baristas started being rude to customers, stopped cleaning the toilets
or on the other hand if they started putting in extra effort, it would
absolutely affects how successful the company is. It seems only fair
that the work of the group of baristas is rewarded in the same way as
upper management is, considering the importance to company of their
hard work.

The comment goes on:

> If the story is "all companies must be fully employee-owned workers'
> cooperatives", then first, note that you are calling for a
> restriction on workers' rights: they have to be given part of their
> pay as stocks, and they can't sell them freely. Second, that will
> probably make markets work worse. There's a large economics
> literature on this: worker-owned cooperatives have not taken over
> the market, although they are an available institutional form,
> because (a) they find it hard to raise capital (b) they tend to make
> decisions that maximize worker welfare rather than profit, e.g. they
> won't sack underperforming divisions or expand in ways that dilute
> existing workers' stake.

I don't really see how granting workers something that they previously
did not have- ownership of the company that they work for, is a
restiction of their rights. Sure, in the situation described, and in
the article, they wouldn't be able to freely sell that ownership to
whoever they like (but instead are simply paid out for their
stake). Workers can't sell ownership *at all* right now, as they don't
have any ownership at all.

Worse, (b) here completely lets the cat out of the bag: *(b) they tend
to make decisions that maximize worker welfare rather than
profit*. Yes. Exactly. That's a good thing! Companies absolutely
*should* tend to prioritise worker welfare over profit. Companies that
tend not to sack people, unless its necessary? Good! Companies
balancing profit against worker welfare sounds like a good thing to
me.

And the very last point: companies will not *expand in ways that
dilute existing workers' stake*. Well, yes, and the same principle
applies *right now*, except instead of protecting workers, it protects
often exceedingly rich investors, who are not personally affected by
the decisions being made, and whose wealth has grown
disproportionately to the mums and dads who work for them. For
example, many existing owners of companies would not like these
changes, and will act in ways which protect their stake. So, I doubt
changes like this will happen at Starbucks soon. If the owners
perceive it as against their financial interest, changes would not
happen even if that were objectively better for all the workers and
their families, for customers or even for the company itself.

Finally, this last point doesn't even fit with the previous
argument. Far from shunning the complexities and financial uncertainty
of ownership, it is now suggested that workers would like their stake
so much they change company policy so that they don't dilute their
ownership, or the powers that gives them. And rightly so, as company
decisions impact their lives, they deserve to have a say, and not have
it dictated by owners whose only interest is financial profit.

If I was the barista, I know which type of company I would prefer to
work for.
